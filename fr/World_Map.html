Sites fossilifères du monde
  • Burgess Shale, British Columbia, Canada — ~508 Ma (Middle Cambrian). A mudslide buried a sea floor so fast that soft bodies, including legs, guts, and eyes, turned to stone. The fossil is Anomalocaris, a top predator. The drawing is five-eyed Opabinia, a different animal from these rocks. Royal Ontario Museum: The Burgess Shale UNESCO World Heritage: Canadian Rocky Mountain Parks (includes the Burgess Shale)
  • Messel Pit, Germany — 47–48 Ma (Middle Eocene). Animals that sank into a deep volcanic lake were preserved whole, with skin, feathers, fur outlines, and even last meals. The fossil is Darwinius, nicknamed Ida. The model is Propalaeotherium, an early horse from the same pit, not the same animal. Messel Pit visitor site UNESCO World Heritage: Messel Pit Fossil Site
  • Dinosaur Provincial Park, Alberta, Canada — 75–77 Ma (Late Cretaceous). Rivers buried dinosaurs in sand and mud on a warm coastal plain. Wind and rain now uncover them in the badlands. The skeleton is Gorgosaurus, a tyrannosaur. The drawing is Centrosaurus, a horned dinosaur. They are different animals from this park. Alberta Parks: Dinosaur Provincial Park UNESCO World Heritage: Dinosaur Provincial Park
  • La Brea Tar Pits, Los Angeles, USA — ~50,000–10,000 years ago (Late Pleistocene). Sticky asphalt trapped Ice Age animals and preserved their bones, including saber-toothed cats, dire wolves, mammoths, and giant ground sloths. Both pictures are a saber-toothed cat (Smilodon). La Brea Tar Pits & Museum
  • Ediacara Hills, South Australia — ~560–542 Ma (Ediacaran). Before animals had shells, bones, or legs, the sea floor was covered in soft creatures shaped like discs, fronds, and ribbons. The fossil is Dickinsonia costata, from these hills. The drawing is Dickinsonia menneri, a related species. UNESCO Tentative List: Flinders Ranges (not inscribed yet)
  • Wadi Al-Hitan, Egypt — ~40–37 Ma (Late Eocene). Whole skeletons of early whales, still with tiny back legs and feet. The photo is a Basilosaurus isis jaw in the rock. The drawing is Basilosaurus cetoides, a related species. UNESCO World Heritage: Wadi Al-Hitan (Whale Valley)
  • Ischigualasto, Argentina — ~231–225 Ma (Late Triassic). These rocks record the Triassic, when the first dinosaurs and the first mammal relatives appeared. The pictures are Eoraptor, a small early meat-eater. The skull is a museum cast, not the original fossil. UNESCO World Heritage: Ischigualasto / Talampaya Natural Parks
  • Monte San Giorgio, Switzerland/Italy — 245–230 Ma (Middle Triassic). Sea reptiles, fish, ammonites, and shrimp-like animals sank into a calm tropical lagoon. The pictures are Tanystropheus, a reptile with a neck longer than its body. UNESCO World Heritage: Monte San Giorgio
  • Mistaken Point, Newfoundland, Canada — 580–560 Ma. Some of the oldest large, complex animal-like life ever found. These are soft, frond-shaped seafloor creatures pressed into rock. UNESCO World Heritage: Mistaken Point
  • Chengjiang Fossil Site, Yunnan, China — ~530 Ma. Animals preserved in amazing detail, sometimes including soft body parts. The pictures are Fuxianhuia protensa, an arthropod from this site. UNESCO World Heritage: Chengjiang Fossil Site
  • Joggins Fossil Cliffs, Nova Scotia, Canada — Carboniferous. Whole fossil trees still stand upright in the cliffs, and some of the earliest reptiles were preserved inside hollow trunks. UNESCO World Heritage: Joggins Fossil Cliffs Parks Canada: Joggins Fossil Cliffs
  • Petrified Forest National Park, Arizona, USA — Late Triassic. Logs that slowly turned into colorful stone (quartz), along with early dinosaurs and their relatives. The drawing is Chindesaurus, an early dinosaur found in this park. National Park Service: petrified wood National Park Service: dinosaurs of Petrified Forest
  • Dinosaur National Monument, Utah/Colorado, USA — Jurassic. A rock wall in the Quarry Exhibit Hall still holds about 1,500 dinosaur bones. The National Park Service names Allosaurus, Apatosaurus, Camarasaurus, Diplodocus, and Stegosaurus among them. National Park Service: Quarry Exhibit Hall
  • Jurassic Coast (Dorset & East Devon), UK — ~185 Myr record. 155 km of coastline whose rock layers hold about 185 million years of Earth history, including marine reptiles such as Ichthyosaurus from Lyme Regis. UNESCO World Heritage: Dorset and East Devon Coast
  • Riversleigh & Naracoorte, Australia — Cenozoic. Fossils of ancient marsupials. The pictures are Nimbadon lavarackorum, a wombat relative from Riversleigh. Naracoorte’s caves also hold ice-age animals, including the marsupial lion. UNESCO World Heritage: Australian Fossil Mammal Sites
  • Cradle of Humankind, South Africa — Plio-Pleistocene. Caves full of fossils of early human relatives, including the Taung Child, a young Australopithecus africanus. UNESCO World Heritage: Fossil Hominid Sites of South Africa
  • EPICA Dome C & Vostok ice cores, Antarctica — ~800,000 years ago → today. Snow that falls in Antarctica never melts. It piles up and squeezes into ice, trapping tiny bubbles of real ancient air. Scientists drilled more than 3 km down at Dome C (EPICA) and Vostok. NOAA: ice-core paleoclimatology EPICA Dome C ice core (Nature) Vostok ice core (Nature)
  • Greenland ice cores (GISP2 / NGRIP), Greenland — ~123,000 years ago → today. On top of Greenland, each year’s snow makes a thin layer, like tree rings made of ice. Drilling projects GISP2 and NGRIP counted and measured those layers. NOAA: ice-core paleoclimatology NGRIP ice core (Nature) Greenland ice-core record (Nature)
  • Hamersley banded iron, Karijini NP, Western Australia — ~2.5 billion years ago. These gorge walls are striped red and grey, layer after layer. The red stripes are iron rust that settled on an ancient sea floor when tiny microbes started making oxygen, and that oxygen rusted iron dissolved in the ocean. Karijini National Park
  • Shark Bay stromatolites (Hamelin Pool), Western Australia — Living today; ancestry ~3.5 billion years. These lumpy rocks in shallow, extra-salty water are alive. Sticky mats of microbes trap sand and grow upward. UNESCO World Heritage: Shark Bay Hamelin Pool (Shark Bay)
  • Jack Hills zircons, Western Australia — ~4.4 billion years ago. Inside these hills are crystals smaller than a grain of sand, called zircons. One was dated to about 4.4 billion years, Earth’s oldest known piece. Oldest Jack Hills zircon (Nature)
  • Barberton Makhonjwa Mountains, South Africa — 3.6–3.25 billion years ago. These mountains hold some of the best-kept rocks from early Earth: old volcanoes, sea floor, and even layers of droplets from giant meteorite impacts. UNESCO World Heritage: Barberton Makhonjwa Mountains
  • Gubbio K-Pg boundary (Bottaccione Gorge), Italy — ~66 million years ago. In a roadside cliff near Gubbio there is a thin clay layer between two kinds of limestone. In 1980 the Alvarez team found it full of iridium, a metal rare on Earth but common in asteroids. See also: Chicxulub impact crater, Yucatán, Mexico. Gubbio (Wikipedia) Iridium at the K-Pg boundary (Science, 1980)
  • Grand Canyon National Park, Arizona, USA — ~1.8 billion years of rock. The Colorado River cut a mile-deep slice through Arizona, so you can see rock layers stacked like pages in a book. The oldest at the bottom are nearly 2 billion years old. Younger layers above record old seas, deserts, and swamps (NPS). NPS: Grand Canyon geology Grand Canyon National Park
  • Acasta Gneiss, Northwest Territories, Canada — ~4.03 billion years ago. On a small island in a remote Canadian river sits some of the oldest rock on Earth — a striped stone called gneiss, about 4.03 billion years old. Scientists dated tiny zircon crystals inside it (Bowring & Williams, 1999). Acasta Gneiss (Wikipedia) Bowring & Williams, 1999
  • Snowball Earth glacial & cap-carbonate rocks, Otavi Mountains, Namibia — ~717–635 million years ago. In northern Namibia, rocks dropped by glaciers sit right under thick pink limestone that forms in warm seas. Paul Hoffman’s team (1998) argued the whole planet froze over — ice nearly to the equator — and then flipped to a hothouse when volcanoes built up enough carbon dioxide. Snowball Earth Hoffman et al., 1998 (PubMed)
  • Elatina Formation tillite, Flinders Ranges, South Australia — ~640–635 million years ago. This jumbled rock is old glacier rubble — boulders and mud dumped by ice. Magnetic clues show it formed close to the equator. Snowball Earth (Wikipedia) Snowball Earth
  • Chicxulub impact crater, Yucatán, Mexico — ~66 million years ago. Buried under Mexico’s Yucatán is a crater about 180 km wide, made when an asteroid roughly 10 km across hit Earth. It was identified in 1991 (Hildebrand et al.), solving the mystery that started with the iridium clay at Gubbio, Italy. See also: Gubbio K-Pg boundary (Bottaccione Gorge), Italy. Chicxulub (Lunar and Planetary Institute) NASA Earth Observatory: Yucatán relief map
