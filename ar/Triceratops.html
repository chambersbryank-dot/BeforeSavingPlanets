Triceratops

Triceratops is the three-horned plant-eater of the last part of the Cretaceous in western North America. The photograph is a mounted skeleton at the Ulster Museum in Belfast. The file page calls it Triceratops horridus. It does not say which quarry the bones came from, so this page does not invent one.

Timeline

  • Erickson and Olson (1996) describe a Triceratops pelvis from the Late Maastrichtian Hell Creek Formation of east-central Montana. That is the end of the Cretaceous, in the same rock unit as Tyrannosaurus rex.

What we know

  • The pelvis is MOR 799, at the Museum of the Rockies in Bozeman. It carries 58 definite bite marks and 22 possible ones. Punctures reach 2.5 cm across and 3.7 cm deep. A cast of one deep bite matches an adult T. rex tooth, including the cutting edge. No other meat-eater known from the Hell Creek fauna was large enough, in that paper’s judgment, to do this. Erickson and Olson, 1996
  • At least 39 punctures are on the underside and the sides of the sacrum, so the pelvis was turned over before or during the biting. The first sacral vertebra is nearly bitten in half. About a sixth of the front of the left ilium was bitten away.

What we don’t know yet

  • Whether the tyrannosaur killed this Triceratops or fed on an animal that was already dead. The paper describes feeding. It does not show the moment of death.
  • Where the Ulster Museum mount was dug up. The file page does not say. This page does not guess a state or a quarry.
  • A GPS point for that bitten pelvis. The 1996 paper says east-central Montana and prints no coordinate. The pins on this map are other Triceratops horridus localities from the Paleobiology Database, not that bone.

Its role in Earth’s ecosystem

The bite marks are a direct link in a food web. An adult Tyrannosaurus rex, or more than one, bit into this Triceratops pelvis hard enough to overturn it and to cut bone. Triceratops was not only a horned animal standing in a landscape. It was flesh in a T. rex meal, at least after death. Erickson and Olson, 1996

The paper does not count how often this happened, and it does not show Triceratops eating. The horns and the beak are visible on the Belfast mount. What plants those jaws cropped is not in the 1996 paper, so this page does not name a diet.
