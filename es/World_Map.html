Yacimientos de fósiles del mundo
  • Burgess Shale, British Columbia, Canada — ~508 Ma (Middle Cambrian). A mudslide buried a sea floor so fast that soft bodies, including legs, guts, and eyes, turned to stone. The fossil is Anomalocaris, a top predator. The drawing is five-eyed Opabinia, a different animal from these rocks. Royal Ontario Museum: The Burgess Shale UNESCO World Heritage: Canadian Rocky Mountain Parks (includes the Burgess Shale)
  • Messel Pit, Germany — 47–48 Ma (Middle Eocene). Animals that sank into a deep volcanic lake were preserved whole, with skin, feathers, fur outlines, and even last meals. The fossil is Darwinius, nicknamed Ida. The model is Propalaeotherium, an early horse from the same pit, not the same animal. Messel Pit visitor site UNESCO World Heritage: Messel Pit Fossil Site
  • Dinosaur Provincial Park, Alberta, Canada — 75–77 Ma (Late Cretaceous). Rivers buried dinosaurs in sand and mud on a warm coastal plain. Wind and rain now uncover them in the badlands. The skeleton is Gorgosaurus, a tyrannosaur. The drawing is Centrosaurus, a horned dinosaur. They are different animals from this park. Alberta Parks: Dinosaur Provincial Park UNESCO World Heritage: Dinosaur Provincial Park
  • La Brea Tar Pits, Los Angeles, USA — ~50,000–10,000 years ago (Late Pleistocene). Sticky asphalt trapped Ice Age animals and preserved their bones, including saber-toothed cats, dire wolves, mammoths, and giant ground sloths. Both pictures are a saber-toothed cat (Smilodon). La Brea Tar Pits & Museum
  • Ediacara Hills, South Australia — ~560–542 Ma (Ediacaran). Before animals had shells, bones, or legs, the sea floor was covered in soft creatures shaped like discs, fronds, and ribbons. The fossil is Dickinsonia costata, from these hills. The drawing is Dickinsonia menneri, a related species. UNESCO Tentative List: Flinders Ranges (not inscribed yet)
  • Wadi Al-Hitan, Egypt — ~40–37 Ma (Late Eocene). Whole skeletons of early whales, still with tiny back legs and feet. The photo is a Basilosaurus isis jaw in the rock. The drawing is Basilosaurus cetoides, a related species. UNESCO World Heritage: Wadi Al-Hitan (Whale Valley)
  • Ischigualasto, Argentina — ~231–225 Ma (Late Triassic). These rocks record the Triassic, when the first dinosaurs and the first mammal relatives appeared. The pictures are Eoraptor, a small early meat-eater. The skull is a museum cast, not the original fossil. UNESCO World Heritage: Ischigualasto / Talampaya Natural Parks
  • Monte San Giorgio, Switzerland/Italy — 245–230 Ma (Middle Triassic). Sea reptiles, fish, ammonites, and shrimp-like animals sank into a calm tropical lagoon. The pictures are Tanystropheus, a reptile with a neck longer than its body. UNESCO World Heritage: Monte San Giorgio
  • Mistaken Point, Newfoundland, Canada — 580–560 Ma. Some of the oldest large, complex animal-like life ever found. These are soft, frond-shaped seafloor creatures pressed into rock. UNESCO World Heritage: Mistaken Point
  • Chengjiang Fossil Site, Yunnan, China — ~530 Ma. Animals preserved in amazing detail, sometimes including soft body parts. The pictures are Fuxianhuia protensa, an arthropod from this site. UNESCO World Heritage: Chengjiang Fossil Site
  • Joggins Fossil Cliffs, Nova Scotia, Canada — Carboniferous. Whole fossil trees still stand upright in the cliffs, and some of the earliest reptiles were preserved inside hollow trunks. UNESCO World Heritage: Joggins Fossil Cliffs Parks Canada: Joggins Fossil Cliffs
  • Petrified Forest National Park, Arizona, USA — Late Triassic. Logs that slowly turned into colorful stone (quartz), along with early dinosaurs and their relatives. The drawing is Chindesaurus, an early dinosaur found in this park. National Park Service: petrified wood National Park Service: dinosaurs of Petrified Forest
  • Dinosaur National Monument, Utah/Colorado, USA — Jurassic. A rock wall in the Quarry Exhibit Hall still holds about 1,500 dinosaur bones. The National Park Service names Allosaurus, Apatosaurus, Camarasaurus, Diplodocus, and Stegosaurus among them. National Park Service: Quarry Exhibit Hall
  • Jurassic Coast (Dorset & East Devon), UK — ~185 Myr record. 155 km of coastline whose rock layers hold about 185 million years of Earth history, including marine reptiles such as Ichthyosaurus from Lyme Regis. UNESCO World Heritage: Dorset and East Devon Coast
  • Riversleigh & Naracoorte, Australia — Cenozoic. Fossils of ancient marsupials. The pictures are Nimbadon lavarackorum, a wombat relative from Riversleigh. Naracoorte’s caves also hold ice-age animals, including the marsupial lion. UNESCO World Heritage: Australian Fossil Mammal Sites
  • Cradle of Humankind, South Africa — Plio-Pleistocene. Caves full of fossils of early human relatives, including the Taung Child, a young Australopithecus africanus. UNESCO World Heritage: Fossil Hominid Sites of South Africa
  • EPICA Dome C & Vostok ice cores, Antarctica — ~800,000 years ago → today. Snow that falls in Antarctica never melts. It piles up and squeezes into ice, trapping tiny bubbles of real ancient air. Scientists drilled more than 3 km down at Dome C (EPICA) and Vostok. NOAA: ice-core paleoclimatology EPICA Dome C ice core (Nature) Vostok ice core (Nature)
  • Greenland ice cores (GISP2 / NGRIP), Greenland — ~123,000 years ago → today. On top of Greenland, each year’s snow makes a thin layer, like tree rings made of ice. Drilling projects GISP2 and NGRIP counted and measured those layers. NOAA: ice-core paleoclimatology NGRIP ice core (Nature) Greenland ice-core record (Nature)
  • Hamersley banded iron, Karijini NP, Western Australia — ~2.5 billion years ago. These gorge walls are striped red and grey, layer after layer. The red stripes are iron rust that settled on an ancient sea floor when tiny microbes started making oxygen, and that oxygen rusted iron dissolved in the ocean. Karijini National Park
  • Shark Bay stromatolites (Hamelin Pool), Western Australia — Living today; ancestry ~3.5 billion years. These lumpy rocks in shallow, extra-salty water are alive. Sticky mats of microbes trap sand and grow upward. UNESCO World Heritage: Shark Bay Hamelin Pool (Shark Bay)
  • Jack Hills zircons, Western Australia — ~4.4 billion years ago. Inside these hills are crystals smaller than a grain of sand, called zircons. One was dated to about 4.4 billion years, Earth’s oldest known piece. Oldest Jack Hills zircon (Nature)
  • Barberton Makhonjwa Mountains, South Africa — 3.6–3.25 billion years ago. These mountains hold some of the best-kept rocks from early Earth: old volcanoes, sea floor, and even layers of droplets from giant meteorite impacts. UNESCO World Heritage: Barberton Makhonjwa Mountains
  • Gubbio K-Pg boundary (Bottaccione Gorge), Italy — ~66 million years ago. In a roadside cliff near Gubbio there is a thin clay layer between two kinds of limestone. In 1980 the Alvarez team found it full of iridium, a metal rare on Earth but common in asteroids. See also: Chicxulub impact crater, Yucatán, Mexico. Gubbio (Wikipedia) Iridium at the K-Pg boundary (Science, 1980)
  • Grand Canyon National Park, Arizona, USA — ~1.8 billion years of rock. The Colorado River cut a mile-deep slice through Arizona, so you can see rock layers stacked like pages in a book. The oldest at the bottom are nearly 2 billion years old. Younger layers above record old seas, deserts, and swamps (NPS). NPS: Grand Canyon geology Grand Canyon National Park
  • Acasta Gneiss, Northwest Territories, Canada — ~4.03 billion years ago. On a small island in a remote Canadian river sits some of the oldest rock on Earth — a striped stone called gneiss, about 4.03 billion years old. Scientists dated tiny zircon crystals inside it (Bowring & Williams, 1999). Acasta Gneiss (Wikipedia) Bowring & Williams, 1999
  • Snowball Earth glacial & cap-carbonate rocks, Otavi Mountains, Namibia — ~717–635 million years ago. In northern Namibia, rocks dropped by glaciers sit right under thick pink limestone that forms in warm seas. Paul Hoffman’s team (1998) argued the whole planet froze over — ice nearly to the equator — and then flipped to a hothouse when volcanoes built up enough carbon dioxide. Snowball Earth Hoffman et al., 1998 (PubMed)
  • Elatina Formation tillite, Flinders Ranges, South Australia — ~640–635 million years ago. This jumbled rock is old glacier rubble — boulders and mud dumped by ice. Magnetic clues show it formed close to the equator. Snowball Earth (Wikipedia) Snowball Earth
  • Chicxulub impact crater, Yucatán, Mexico — ~66 million years ago. Buried under Mexico’s Yucatán is a crater about 180 km wide, made when an asteroid roughly 10 km across hit Earth. It was identified in 1991 (Hildebrand et al.), solving the mystery that started with the iridium clay at Gubbio, Italy. See also: Gubbio K-Pg boundary (Bottaccione Gorge), Italy. Chicxulub (Lunar and Planetary Institute) NASA Earth Observatory: Yucatán relief map
  • Dob’s Linn, Moffat, Scotland, UK — ~445–443 million years ago (end-Ordovician). In a steep Scottish gully, dark shale is packed with graptolites — tiny floating sea animals that look like pencil marks or saw blades. Ordovician–Silurian extinction (Wikipedia) Global boundary markers (International Commission on Stratigraphy)
  • Coumiac Quarry (Frasnian–Famennian boundary), Montagne Noire, France — ~372 million years ago (Late Devonian). This old red-marble quarry in southern France holds the world’s official marker for the Late Devonian extinction (Klapper et al., 1993). Late Devonian extinction (Wikipedia) Coumiac boundary (Klapper et al., 1993)
  • Siberian Traps, Putorana Plateau, Russia — ~252 million years ago. These huge flat-topped mountains are made of lava — stack after stack of it, covering an area bigger than western Europe. Siberian Traps (Wikipedia) Timing of the eruptions (Burgess and Bowring, 2015)
  • Karoo Basin (Lystrosaurus beds), Eastern Cape, South Africa — ~252–250 million years ago. The dry Karoo has rock layers that run straight through the Great Dying on land. Below the line lived many kinds of strange reptile-like ancestors of mammals. Above it, almost everywhere you dig, one animal turns up: Lystrosaurus, a pig-sized plant-eater with two tusks and a beak. Lystrosaurus (Wikipedia) Karoo extinction layers (Smith and Ward, 2001)
  • Palisades Sill (CAMP lavas), New Jersey, USA — ~201 million years ago (end-Triassic). The tall cliffs across the Hudson River from New York City are cooled magma. They formed when the supercontinent Pangaea began tearing apart to make the Atlantic Ocean. Central Atlantic magmatic province (Wikipedia) CAMP and the end-Triassic (Blackburn et al., 2013)
  • Deccan Traps, Western Ghats, Maharashtra, India — ~66 million years ago (end-Cretaceous). The step-shaped hills of western India are giant lava flows that erupted in pulses around the time the dinosaurs died. Deccan Traps (Wikipedia) Lava dates, team one (Schoene et al., 2019) Lava dates, team two (Sprain et al., 2019)
  • Rhynie Chert, Aberdeenshire, Scotland, UK — ~410 million years ago (Early Devonian). Near the village of Rhynie, hot springs once bubbled like Yellowstone’s. Their silica-rich water flooded a marsh and turned it to glassy stone so fast that tiny plants, fungi, and bugs were frozen cell by cell. The rock shows Horneophyton. The drawing is Aglaophyton, a different plant from the same marsh. These plants were ankle-high, with no leaves and no roots like ours — just green stems. University of Aberdeen: The Rhynie Chert Rhynie chert (Wikipedia)
  • Baragwanathia beds, Yea, Victoria, Australia — ~420–410 million years ago (Late Silurian–Early Devonian). In Victoria’s hills, the rocks hold Baragwanathia, a clubmoss with tiny needle-like leaves. It looks like a green pipe cleaner. Both rounds are fossils of that plant. The second photo is another specimen in a museum, not a drawing of it alive. Museums Victoria: Baragwanathia longifolia Baragwanathia (Wikipedia)
  • Gilboa Fossil Forest (and nearby Cairo forest), New York, USA — ~385 million years ago (Middle Devonian). Workers digging a dam in 1870 found stone tree stumps from one of Earth’s earliest forests. The trees, called Wattieza or Eospermatopteris, looked like giant palm-shaped feather dusters, at least 8 metres tall, but they made spores, not seeds. A nearby quarry at Cairo, New York, holds even older roots. Gilboa stumps (Stein and others, 2007) Cairo forest roots (Stein and others, 2020) Gilboa Fossil Forest (Wikipedia)
  • Kiltorcan Old Quarry, County Kilkenny, Ireland — ~360 million years ago (latest Devonian). Yellow sandstone at Kiltorcan holds fronds of Archaeopteris hibernica. The photo is a fossil frond of that species, on display in a museum. The drawing shows a related species, Archaeopteris macilenta, from New York, so you can picture the whole tree. It is an artist’s concept, not a photograph, and it is not the Irish fossil. Geological Survey Ireland: Kiltorcan Old Quarry Age of the Kiltorcan flora (Higgs and others, 1990) Archaeopteris, the earliest known modern tree (Meyer-Berthaud and others, 1999)
  • Fossil Grove, Victoria Park, Glasgow, Scotland, UK — ~330 million years ago (Carboniferous). In a Glasgow park, 11 stone tree stumps still stand exactly where they grew. They were scale trees, Lepidodendron — giant clubmosses with bark like snake skin. The drawing is an artist’s concept of the whole tree, not a photo of this park. Fossil Grove Glasgow The trees at Fossil Grove Fossil Grove (Wikipedia)
  • Glossopteris forests, Transantarctic Mountains, Antarctica — ~270–252 million years ago (Permian). Explorers on Scott’s 1912 expedition hauled Glossopteris leaf fossils off Antarctic rock. The photo is a tongue-shaped leaf from Antarctic rock in a museum, not one of those original samples. The other round is a map of where the same kind of leaf turns up, not a drawing of the living tree. USGS: Glossopteris and continental drift USGS: rejoined continents Glossopteris (Wikipedia)
  • Sirius Passet, Peary Land, North Greenland — ~518 million years ago (Early Cambrian). Closer to the North Pole than almost any fossil site, these rocks hold sea animals from the start of the Cambrian explosion, when animals suddenly got legs, shells, and eyes. Some were armored slugs called Halkieria. One swimmer, Kerygmachela, is a cousin of today’s tiny water bears. The photo is a set of real fossils from the site. The drawing is an artist’s concept of Kerygmachela, not a photograph. Sirius Passet review (Harper and others, 2019) Kerygmachela’s brain and eyes (Park and others, 2018) Sirius Passet (Wikipedia)
  • Emu Bay Shale, Kangaroo Island, South Australia — ~514 million years ago (Early Cambrian). On a beach cliff on Kangaroo Island, scientists found fossil eyes. They belonged to Anomalocaris, a hunter about a metre long, with grabbing arms. People sometimes call it the T. rex of the Cambrian sea. Each eye had more than 16,000 tiny lenses, sharper than most insect eyes today. The other round is a museum model of Anomalocaris canadensis, a Burgess Shale species. It is an Anomalocaris relative, not the Kangaroo Island animal, and it is a photo of a model. Fossil eyes of Anomalocaris (Paterson and others, 2011) Emu Bay Shale (Wikipedia)
  • Fezouata Formation, Zagora, Morocco — ~478 million years ago (Early Ordovician). Desert hills near Zagora were once a cold seafloor near the South Pole. They hold soft-bodied animals that scientists thought had vanished after the Cambrian. The star is Aegirocassis, a giant about two metres long that filtered tiny food from the water, a bit like a whale does, long before whales existed. The photo is that fossil. The drawing is an artist’s concept of the living animal, not a photograph. Giant filter-feeder Aegirocassis (Van Roy, Daley, and Briggs, 2015) Fezouata Formation (Wikipedia)
  • Mazon Creek fossil beds, Grundy/Will Counties, Illinois, USA — ~309 million years ago (Pennsylvanian / Late Carboniferous). Crack open a round ironstone pebble from these Illinois creeks and you might find a fern leaf, a jellyfish, or the Tully Monster. That animal was a strange swimmer with eyes on a stiff bar and a claw on a long snout. It is Illinois’s state fossil. Scientists still argue over what it was. A 2016 study said it was an early backboned animal. Later studies, including one in 2023, disagreed and said the evidence does not show that. The drawing is an artist’s concept of one idea of how it looked. It is not a settled picture and not a photograph. A 2016 claim that it was a backboned animal (McCoy and others) Mazon Creek fossil beds (Wikipedia)
  • Solnhofen Limestone, Bavaria, Germany — ~150 million years ago (Late Jurassic). Southern Germany was once a string of salty, warm lagoons. The bottom water had almost no oxygen, so anything that sank was left alone, then covered in fine lime mud. That is how Archaeopteryx was preserved, feathers and all. It had feathers, teeth, and a long bony tail. The photo is the Berlin specimen, the original fossil, not a cast. The drawing is an artist’s concept of Archaeopteryx chasing Compsognathus, a small dinosaur from the same lagoons. The drawing is not a photograph. Bürgermeister-Müller-Museum, Solnhofen Museum für Naturkunde Berlin: the Berlin specimen Solnhofen Limestone (Wikipedia)
  • Jehol Biota (Yixian Formation), Liaoning, China — ~125 million years ago (Early Cretaceous). Volcanoes near ancient lakes in northeast China kept burying forests and lakeshores in fine ash, a bit like Pompeii, but for dinosaurs. The ash saved fuzzy, feathered dinosaurs. Scientists found tiny color sacs in the fuzz of Sinosauropteryx. A 2017 study used those sacs to work out a ginger back, a white belly, and a striped tail. The photo is a fossil of that dinosaur on display. The drawing is an artist’s concept of those colors, not a photograph. Color sacs in dinosaur feathers (Zhang and others, 2010) Ginger back and striped tail (Smithwick and others, 2017) Jehol Biota (Wikipedia)
  • Qujing, Yunnan, China (Kuanti Formation) — ~423 million years ago (Late Silurian). In southern China, scientists found Guiyu, one of the oldest near-complete fossils of a bony fish. It was about 33 centimetres long, the length of a school ruler. Your skeleton is made of bone because of fish like this. Guiyu mixes features of ray-finned fish, such as a goldfish, and lobe-finned fish, the group that later crawled onto land. The photo is one piece of a Guiyu fossil, mostly the ridged scales. The bright colors on that sheet are a map of the bones, not the colors of the fish. The other round is an artist’s concept of the whole animal, not a photograph. Guiyu, an early bony fish (Zhu and others, 2009) Guiyu oneiros (Wikipedia)
  • Gogo Formation, Kimberley, Western Australia — ~380 million years ago (Late Devonian). Gogo was an ancient reef. Fish were buried so gently that their bones are still 3-D, and some even kept muscles and nerves. One fish, Materpiscis, means mother fish. It was found with an embryo inside and a mineralized umbilical cord, the oldest known live birth in a backboned animal. This card does not have a clear photo of that fossil. The Western Australian Museum keeps the real specimen. One round is a photo of a model at Museums Victoria. The other is a low-resolution restoration of the birth, with a cord to the baby. Neither picture is a photograph of the fossil. The Gogo mother fish (Long and others, 2008) Gogo Formation (Wikipedia)
  • Miguasha National Park, Quebec, Canada (UNESCO) — ~380 million years ago (Late Devonian). Miguasha’s cliffs hold an ancient estuary full of fish. The star is Eusthenopteron, the Prince of Miguasha. Its fin bones are arranged like your arm: one bone, then two, then little ones. The photo is a young Eusthenopteron on display at the Redpath Museum. The other round is an artist’s concept of the living fish, not a photograph. UNESCO World Heritage: Miguasha National Park Eusthenopteron (Wikipedia)
  • Cleveland Shale, Cleveland, Ohio, USA — ~360 million years ago (Late Devonian). Before animals with legs were common, the seas had armored hunters. Dunkleosteus, from the shale under Cleveland, wore a bony helmet and had self-sharpening jaw blades instead of teeth. The photo is skull CMNH 5768 at the Cleveland Museum of Natural History. The skull and the belly armor are real. Some of the side plates are rebuilt. The other round is an artist’s concept of the whole fish, not a photograph. Newer studies picture a shorter body than the old museum monsters. Dunkleosteus armor (Engelman, 2024) Cleveland Shale (Wikipedia)
  • Bird Fiord (Tiktaalik), Ellesmere Island, Nunavut, Canada — ~375 million years ago (Late Devonian). In 2004, after years of searching Arctic rocks of just the right age, scientists found Tiktaalik, a fishapod. It had scales and gills like a fish, but also a neck, a flat crocodile-like head, and sturdy fins with wrist bones that could prop it up in shallow water. The photo is a cast at the Field Museum in Chicago, not the original fossil. The originals are kept for Nunavut. The other round is an artist’s concept, not a photograph. Tiktaalik (Daeschler, Shubin, and Jenkins, 2006) Tiktaalik (Wikipedia)
  • Stensiö Bjerg, Gauss Halvø, East Greenland — ~365 million years ago (Late Devonian). East Greenland gave us two of the first animals with real legs and toes. Acanthostega had eight fingers on each hand and still used gills. Those legs were for paddling, not for walking on land. Ichthyostega was chunkier, with strong shoulders that could help it haul itself along. The skull photo is Acanthostega on display in Munich, and it may be a cast. The other round is a museum model of Acanthostega in Stuttgart. It is a model, not a photograph of the living animal. Ichthyostega is not in the pictures. Acanthostega skull (Clack, 2002) Acanthostega (Wikipedia)
  • Red Hill, Pennsylvania, USA (Catskill Formation) — ~365 million years ago (Late Devonian). A road cut in Pennsylvania preserves a warm river world with early forests, fish, and Hynerpeton. Hynerpeton is one of the first four-legged animals known from North America, and it is known from a strong shoulder bone, not a whole skeleton. Edward Daeschler and others described that shoulder bone in 1994. No legs or toes of this animal have been found. The photo is that shoulder bone, on display at the Academy of Natural Sciences. The other round is an artist’s concept of a whole animal. The head, legs, and toes in the drawing are a guess. It is not a photograph. Hynerpeton (Wikipedia)
  • Windjana Gorge, Kimberley, Western Australia — More than 375 million years ago (Devonian). These cliffs were once a reef in a warm sea. Sponge-like animals called stromatoporoids, plus algae, built most of it. Corals lived here too, but they were not the main builders the way they are on today’s Great Barrier Reef. The sea is gone. In the dry season you can walk in the gorge the Lennard River cut through that reef. The close-up is a stromatoporoid from Ohio, not a fossil collected in this gorge. Windjana Gorge National Park (Wikipedia) Parks guide: geology of Windjana, Geikie, and Tunnel Creek
  • Capitan Reef, Guadalupe Mountains, Texas, USA — ~265–260 million years ago (Middle Permian). El Capitan is the top of one of the best-preserved fossil reefs on Earth. Sponges, algae, and lacy bryozoans built it at the edge of an inland sea. Later the land rose and the reef became a mountain. Acidic water then dissolved caves in that reef rock. Carlsbad Caverns, in New Mexico, is one of those caves. National Park Service: geologic formations National Park Service: fossils of Guadalupe Mountains
  • Wren’s Nest, Dudley, England — ~430–427 million years ago (Silurian, Wenlock). Dudley sat on a shallow tropical reef, south of the equator. The limestone is packed with corals, sea lilies, and trilobites. One trilobite, Calymene blumenbachii, was so common that workers called it the Dudley Bug, and it went on the town’s coat of arms. Ripples from the old seabed are still in the rock. The fossil is a Dudley Bug from a quarry at Wren’s Nest. The other round is a museum diorama of a Silurian seafloor in Ohio. It shows sea scorpions. It is not this reef. Wren’s Nest (Wikipedia) Dudley Bug, Calymene blumenbachii (Wikipedia)
  • Gotland, Sweden (Silurian reefs) — ~440–420 million years ago (Silurian). Gotland is mostly old reef limestone. Waves wore that hard rock into sea stacks called raukar. The photo is one rauk at Lergrav, on the northeast coast, with a hole worn through it. Chain corals called Halysites also turn up in these rocks. Their tiny tubes join like links in a necklace. The coral round is a Halysites from Gotland. Gotland (Wikipedia) Rauk (Wikipedia) Halysites, chain coral (Wikipedia)
  • Falls of the Ohio, Indiana, USA — ~390 million years ago (Middle Devonian). When the Ohio River is low, it uncovers a huge bed of fossil corals. Horn corals and other corals still sit in the Jeffersonville Limestone, in the places where they grew, in a warm shallow sea. Back then, this part of North America was close to the equator. Falls of the Ohio State Park (Wikipedia) Falls of the Ohio visitor site Jeffersonville Limestone (Wikipedia)
  • Great Barrier Reef, Queensland, Australia — Today (reef structures ~600,000 years; living reef ~9,000 years). This is the biggest coral reef on Earth today, and the newest page in a reef story hundreds of millions of years long. Older reefs on this map were built mostly by sponges, algae, and other animals. This one is built mostly by corals. Like those old reefs, it stores a record of the sea in its skeletons. Heat waves are now bleaching it. UNESCO World Heritage: Great Barrier Reef Great Barrier Reef (Wikipedia) NASA Earth Observatory: heat stress on the Great Barrier Reef Saving Planets: coral reef restoration
